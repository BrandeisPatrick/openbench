## 1. Main empirically supported models of human debugging and program comprehension

### Takeaway
The best-measured process findings on professional developers working in unfamiliar real code are (i) navigation and searching dominate effort and most searches fail (Ko et al. 2006), (ii) during debugging, developers verbalize cue/scent-following about four times as often as hypotheses, form only one or two hypotheses per bug and mostly modify rather than replace them, and half of hypotheses are abandoned (Lawrance et al. 2013), and (iii) in naturalistic sessions debugging time is spread across edit/test/browse/inspect with no single dominant activity, and state inspection is mostly logging, not debuggers (Alaboudi & LaToza). Older lab work supplies the strategy constructs (systematic vs as-needed; top-down/bottom-up; expert hypothesis quality) but with small samples and toy-to-medium programs.

### Cited Findings

**Information foraging theory (IFT) in debugging — Lawrance, Bogart, Burnett, Bellamy, Rector, Fleming, "How Programmers Debug, Revisited: An Information Foraging Theory Perspective", IEEE TSE 39(2):197–215, 2013, DOI 10.1109/TSE.2010.111 [FULL]**
- Constructs: bug = "prey"; the program's navigable links = "topology"; words/identifiers visible on screen = "cues"; "information scent" = perceived likelihood a cue leads to prey (operationalised as word similarity between the bug report and the code around a cue, propagated by spreading activation over topology in the PFIS model). — [Lawrance et al. 2013 PDF](https://web.engr.oregonstate.edu/~burnett/Reprints/TSE-IFT-2013-asprinted.pdf)
- Method: think-aloud with synchronized audio/video/screen capture plus an IDE action log; 12 IBM professional Java programmers recruited, 10 analysed (one pilot, one recording failure); subject program RSSOwl (193 classes, 80,520 LOC); two then-open real bug reports (a "Broken Feature" code-level defect and a "Missing Feature" spec-level defect); 1 hour per bug; bugs were chosen to be unsolvable in the session because navigation, not fixing, was the object of study. — [same](https://web.engr.oregonstate.edu/~burnett/Reprints/TSE-IFT-2013-asprinted.pdf)
- Coding: hypothesis codes = start / modify / confirm / abandon; scent codes = scent-to-seek / scent-gained / scent-lost; inter-rater agreement 97% (99% excluding "no code"). — [same](https://web.engr.oregonstate.edu/~burnett/Reprints/TSE-IFT-2013-asprinted.pdf)
- Results: scent verbalizations outnumbered hypothesis verbalizations by a mean factor of 4.14 (paired t-test on log counts, p < 0.000004, df = 9), and this held for every participant on both bugs; participants expressed "few new hypotheses: sometimes only one or two original hypotheses per bug"; half of hypothesis starts were later abandoned; hypotheses were modified 5.57× (BF) and 7.16× (MF) more often than started; scent was gained 3.7× more often than lost, and sought 4.4× more often than lost; scent gains were often serendipitous. — [same](https://web.engr.oregonstate.edu/~burnett/Reprints/TSE-IFT-2013-asprinted.pdf)
- Predictive model: for the BF bug, PFIS (bug-report scent + topology) predicted next navigation significantly better than bug-report cosine similarity alone (N = 3,002 navigations, p < 0.00001), with recall approaching 100% at a 25–30% false-positive rate; for the MF (feature-request-like) bug, plain cosine similarity beat PFIS. Bug reports predicted navigation better than participants' own verbalizations. — [same](https://web.engr.oregonstate.edu/~burnett/Reprints/TSE-IFT-2013-asprinted.pdf)
- The paper restates the Ko et al. figure that programmers spend 35% of their time navigating, and argues hypothesis-based theories (Brooks top-down; Letovsky bottom-up conjectures) give tool builders little practical guidance compared with scent. — [same](https://web.engr.oregonstate.edu/~burnett/Reprints/TSE-IFT-2013-asprinted.pdf)

**Seek–relate–collect model — Ko, Myers, Coblenz, Aung, "An Exploratory Study of How Developers Seek, Relate, and Collect Relevant Information during Software Maintenance Tasks", IEEE TSE 32(12):971–987, 2006 [FULL]** (DOI commonly given as 10.1109/TSE.2006.116; not confirmed on the PDF I read)
- Method: screen-captured lab sessions, transcribed into actions; 31 Java developers recruited, the 10 most experienced analysed (7 self-described Java experts); unfamiliar "Paint" Java program; 2 debugging + 3 enhancement tasks in 70 minutes, with experimenter-injected interruptions (22% ±6 of time, excluded from proportions). — [Ko et al. 2006 PDF](https://faculty.washington.edu/ajko/papers/Ko2006SeekRelateCollect.pdf)
- Time allocation (non-interrupted time): about a fifth reading code, a fifth editing, a quarter textual search + dependency navigation, a tenth testing the application; 5% (±2) switching/reorienting; 6% (±4) reading API docs (almost always in the web browser). Developers finished 3.4 (±0.8) of 5 tasks. — [same](https://faculty.washington.edu/ajko/papers/Ko2006SeekRelateCollect.pdf)
- Searches: 88% (±11) of searches led to nothing of later use; about 25 (±9) minutes (~36%) were spent inspecting irrelevant code; on debugging tasks developers searched by surface symptoms (8 of 9 on one task searched "paint" and landed on a method not responsible for the bug) — the "vocabulary problem". — [same](https://faculty.washington.edu/ajko/papers/Ko2006SeekRelateCollect.pdf)
- Navigation: 58% (±20) of navigations followed direct dependencies, 42% (±20) indirect; 27% (±13) of direct and 34% (±23) of indirect navigations returned to recently viewed code (revisits, often back-and-forth comparison while editing). Developers rarely used structured navigation tools (Open Declaration/Java Search) and preferred find/replace. Total navigation mechanics = 19 minutes, i.e. 35% of non-interrupted time. — [same](https://faculty.washington.edu/ajko/papers/Ko2006SeekRelateCollect.pdf)
- Model: developers interleave *seeking* relevant code, *relating* it via dependencies, and *collecting* it (encoding relevance in tabs/scroll positions, which was then lost and re-found). — [same](https://faculty.washington.edu/ajko/papers/Ko2006SeekRelateCollect.pdf)

**Debugging episodes in the wild — Alaboudi & LaToza, "An Exploratory Study of Debugging Episodes", arXiv 2105.02162 (2021); journal version "What constitutes debugging?" EMSE 2023 [FULL for arXiv version; journal version ABS]**
- Method: coded live-streamed think-aloud programming videos; 15 sessions, 11 professional developers (7–31 years OSS, median 9), ~30 hours, projects incl. Firefox, curl, SerenityOS, Webpack, Alacritty; 89 debugging episodes, 2,137 debugging activities, 1,407 programming activities. Activity codes: browse file, edit file, test program (observe output), inspect program state (logs or debugger), consult external resources, other. — [arXiv 2105.02162](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/2105.02162)
- Results: debugging started after 8 (±10) minutes of programming on average ("once every eight minutes"); debugging was 13–95% (avg 48%) of session time; reported defects took 51 (±38) min vs 6 (±8) min for self-inserted defects; distribution highly skewed (long episodes ≥12.3 min = 23 episodes = 80% of debugging time). Editing ~40% and testing ~34–40% of debugging time (the text and Table 4 disagree on the testing figure, per my read), browsing ~12%, inspecting state ~8%, consulting resources ~3%; "no single activity dominated". Inspecting state was 70% log statements vs 30% debugger use. Compared with ordinary programming, developers switched to testing ~2× and inspected state ~6× more often when debugging; browsing/editing shares were similar. — [same](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/2105.02162)
- Dataset: authors state the entire dataset (videos and codes) is public — replication package at https://figshare.com/s/6c4026a941db49ea3de3; no explicit dataset licence given (source videos mostly Creative Commons). — [same](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/2105.02162)
- Related (ABS): Alaboudi & LaToza "Using Hypotheses as a Debugging Aid" reportedly found developers held about two hypotheses per defect and that offering candidate hypotheses made success about six times more likely — from a search snippet only, not verified. — [search snippet, no primary URL checked]

**Systematic vs as-needed strategy — Littman, Pinto, Letovsky, Soloway, "Mental models and software maintenance", J. Systems and Software 7:341–355, 1987 (often cited 1986, Empirical Studies of Programmers) [ABS]**
- Videotaped protocols of experienced programmers enhancing a small personnel-database program; *systematic* programmers traced data flow globally before modifying and succeeded; *as-needed* programmers studied only local code and failed, attributed to missing interactions with code elsewhere (strong causal vs weak static mental models). Later work (Singer & Lethbridge) notes the systematic strategy is unrealistic at scale. Sample size not verified. — [typeset.io record](https://typeset.io/papers/mental-models-and-software-maintenance-3mauac5iji)

**Methodical investigation — Robillard, Coelho, Murphy, "How Effective Developers Investigate Source Code: An Exploratory Study", IEEE TSE 30(12):889–903, 2004, DOI 10.1109/TSE.2004.101 [ABS]**
- Five developers, one change task on a medium-sized OSS system; detailed analysis of which code each examined and how they navigated; more successful developers investigated in a structured, methodical way (and inattention blindness — skimming without registering — was observed). — [PDF host](https://www.st.cs.uni-saarland.de/edu/empirical-se/2006/PDFs/robillard04.pdf) (abstract-level only; I did not read the PDF)

**Top-down / bottom-up / integrated comprehension — von Mayrhauser & Vans, "Program Comprehension During Software Maintenance and Evolution", IEEE Computer, 1995 [ABS]**
- Reviews six comprehension models (Soloway–Adelson–Ehrlich top-down, Pennington bottom-up, etc.) and proposes an integrated metamodel; later large-code studies by the same authors report comprehension switches between top-down and bottom-up. I could not confirm from what I read that the metamodel itself uses the term "opportunistic" (that term is associated with Letovsky). — [PDF host](https://www.cs.kent.edu/~jmaletic/cs69995-PC/papers/von_mayrhauser-1995.pdf)
- Lawrance et al. [FULL] summarise Brooks (hypothesis hierarchy verified by "beacons") and Letovsky (bottom-up conjectures, questions) as the hypothesis-centred tradition IFT reacts against. — [Lawrance et al.](https://web.engr.oregonstate.edu/~burnett/Reprints/TSE-IFT-2013-asprinted.pdf)

**Expert vs novice — Gugerty & Olson, "Debugging by Skilled and Novice Programmers", CHI 1986 [ABS]**
- Two experiments (LOGO, then Pascal); experts debugged faster and more accurately mainly because they generated high-quality hypotheses after less study of the code; novices often introduced new bugs while debugging. Reported sample (LOGO: 18 novices, 6 experts) comes from a secondary source; DOI appears in two forms (10.1145/22339.22367 / 10.1145/22627.22367) — check before citing. — [academia.edu record](https://www.academia.edu/102488941/Debugging_by_skilled_and_novice_programmers)

**Time allocation in the field — Xia, Bao, Lo, Xing, Hassan, Li, "Measuring Program Comprehension: A Large-Scale Field Study with Professionals", IEEE TSE 44(10):951–976, 2018 [ABS]**
- Activity tracking of 78 professionals on 7 projects, 3,148 working hours: ~58% of time on comprehension; senior developers spend a smaller share on comprehension than juniors. — [Monash record](https://research.monash.edu/en/publications/measuring-program-comprehension-a-large-scale-field-study-with-pr/)

**Practitioner debugging benchmark — Böhme, Soremekun, Chattopadhyay, Ugherughe, Zeller, "Where Is the Bug and How Is It Fixed? An Experiment with Practitioners", ESEC/FSE 2017 [ABS]**
- 12 professional engineers (per a companion poster) spent about a month localizing, explaining and fixing 27 real errors in open-source C programs; consolidated fault locations, diagnoses and patches released as DBGBench; examined debugging time, difficulty and strategies across practitioners and error types. — [CISPA record](https://publications.cispa.saarland/1468) (full PDF redirected and was not read)

### Inferences
- The two best-measured constructs for agent-trace transfer are IFT scent-following (Lawrance) and seek/relate/collect with revisit and failed-search rates (Ko); both were measured from action logs plus think-aloud on unfamiliar real code, which is structurally closest to an agent dropped into a SWE-bench repo.
- Human baselines worth quoting carefully (small n, 70-minute or 1-hour lab sessions, Java/Eclipse, 2004–2013): ~35% of time on navigation mechanics; ~88% of searches fruitless; ~27–34% of navigations are revisits; 1–2 hypotheses per bug with ~50% abandoned; scent talk ~4× hypothesis talk.
- The human literature itself is split on hypothesis-driven debugging: classic work (Gugerty & Olson; Brooks) treats hypothesis quality as the expert advantage, while IFT finds hypotheses rare relative to cue-following. An agent whose reasoning text is hypothesis-heavy is therefore not "more human" by default.

### Gaps
- I did not read Robillard 2004, Littman 1987, von Mayrhauser & Vans 1995, Gugerty & Olson 1986, Xia 2018 or Böhme 2017 in full; their sample sizes and numbers above are abstract- or secondary-source-level.
- Not searched: Sillito et al. 2008 (questions programmers ask during change tasks), Minelli et al. ICPC 2015 (IDE time allocation), Katz & Anderson 1987, Vessey 1985, Eisenstadt 1997, eye-tracking work (e.g. Siegmund, Busjahn), Piorkowski/Fleming later IFT papers (e.g. foraging cost/value, "patch" switching). These would add constructs but I cannot cite numbers for them.
- Ko et al. 2006 DOI not confirmed from the PDF text I extracted.

## 2. Constructs with observable trace signatures in an agent command log

### Takeaway
Most human constructs above were themselves measured from action logs (navigation events, searches, edits, test runs), so they port to agent traces with modest operational choices; the ones that need the reasoning text (hypothesis start/modify/abandon, scent-sought vs scent-gained) are portable as *presence* codes only, and Lawrance's PFIS gives a ready-made, text-free predictive model of navigation that can be run on agent traces.

### Cited Findings (human measurement → possible agent signature)
- **Navigation share / navigation-to-edit ratio.** Humans: ~35% of time on navigation mechanics; time split ≈ 1/5 read, 1/5 edit, 1/4 search+navigate, 1/10 test (Ko 2006, time-coded screen video). Agent analogue: share of commands that are `grep/find/rg/ls/cat/sed -n` vs edits vs test runs, by count and by tokens. — [Ko et al. 2006](https://faculty.washington.edu/ajko/papers/Ko2006SeekRelateCollect.pdf)
- **Failed-search rate.** Humans: 88% of searches yielded nothing later used (Ko). Agent analogue: fraction of search commands whose returned files/lines are never subsequently opened, edited, or mentioned. — [Ko et al. 2006](https://faculty.washington.edu/ajko/papers/Ko2006SeekRelateCollect.pdf)
- **Revisits.** Humans: 27% (direct) and 34% (indirect) of navigations returned to recently viewed code (Ko). Agent analogue: re-reads of the same file/line range within a window of N steps (distinguishing productive comparison from loops). — [Ko et al. 2006](https://faculty.washington.edu/ajko/papers/Ko2006SeekRelateCollect.pdf)
- **Irrelevant-code time.** Humans: ~36% of time inspecting code not related to the fix (Ko). Agent analogue: share of file reads outside the gold-patch files and their direct dependencies (needs gold patch). — [Ko et al. 2006](https://faculty.washington.edu/ajko/papers/Ko2006SeekRelateCollect.pdf)
- **Scent-following predictability.** Humans: PFIS (issue-text similarity + link topology) predicted next navigation with recall near 100% at 25–30% FPR on a code-level bug, but lost to plain text similarity on a feature-request-like bug (Lawrance). Agent analogue: rank of each actually-opened file under issue-text TF-IDF/embedding similarity, with and without import-graph propagation; a direct "how issue-text-driven is this agent's navigation" score. — [Lawrance et al. 2013](https://web.engr.oregonstate.edu/~burnett/Reprints/TSE-IFT-2013-asprinted.pdf)
- **Hypothesis lifecycle.** Humans: 1–2 hypotheses per bug, ~50% abandoned, modified 5.6–7.2× as often as started (Lawrance, coded from think-aloud). Agent analogue: code reasoning text for hypothesis start/modify/confirm/abandon and check whether a start is followed by a discriminating command (test, print, read). Text-dependent; presence-only. — [Lawrance et al. 2013](https://web.engr.oregonstate.edu/~burnett/Reprints/TSE-IFT-2013-asprinted.pdf)
- **Episode structure and state inspection.** Humans: debugging episodes recognisable from "error appears / output unexpected / defect reproduced" to "error disappears"; state inspection 70% logging vs 30% debugger (Alaboudi & LaToza). Agent analogue: episode boundaries from failing→passing test or traceback→clean run; print-statement insertion and removal as the logging analogue; debugger invocation (pdb/jdb) rate. — [Alaboudi & LaToza](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/2105.02162)
- **Reproduction before fix.** Agent-side precedent already exists: Ceka et al. measure whether agents generate a reproducer and whether it actually fails before the patch (trigger rate 41–43% Agentless, 55–57% SWE-Agent) and whether it overfits (passes reproducer, fails ground truth: 3.6–4.8% Agentless, 17.9–29.8% SWE-Agent). — [Ceka et al., arXiv 2506.08311](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.08311)
- **Systematic vs as-needed.** Humans: global data-flow tracing before modification vs local-only reading (Littman, ABS). Agent analogue: breadth of reading before first edit (distinct files/functions read, reads outside the edited file's call neighbourhood) — a "pre-edit exploration breadth" metric. — [Littman et al. record](https://typeset.io/papers/mental-models-and-software-maintenance-3mauac5iji)
- **Pivoting and returning to abandoned approaches** (ML development, not debugging): TraceML labels each code version with action/intent/magnitude/score effect and measures pivot rate and return-to-earlier-approach rate; it releases a toolkit converting any command-line agent run into its schema. — [TraceML, arXiv 2608.26086](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.26086)

### Inferences
- Concrete computable features for openbench, each with a human anchor: (1) navigation/edit/test command shares; (2) failed-search rate; (3) revisit rate within a window; (4) off-gold-file read share; (5) PFIS-style issue-scent rank of opened files; (6) pre-edit exploration breadth (systematic vs as-needed); (7) reproduction-before-fix and reproducer-trigger rate; (8) print-debug insert/remove cycles vs debugger use; (9) hypothesis start→discriminating-test adjacency (text-dependent).
- Human numbers are time-based (minutes of video); agent logs are step- or token-based. Any human/agent ratio comparison needs a stated unit conversion, and probably should compare *shapes* (rank order of activity shares, revisit structure) rather than raw percentages.

### Gaps
- No paper found that has already run PFIS or any IFT model on LLM-agent navigation logs.
- No human study found that measures "reproduction before fix" as an explicit rate on SWE-bench-style issues.

## 3. Studies directly comparing humans and LLM coding agents on process

### Takeaway
Direct human-vs-agent *process* comparisons on repository bug fixing are still almost absent. The one strong process comparison I found is on ML development (TraceML: humans pivot and revisit abandoned work; agents fall into narrow loops). On SWE-bench, human comparison is limited to gold patches (scope/size/clone similarity), not human trajectories. Debugging-specific comparisons are small (DePro, 100 Codeforces submissions) or rest on asserted rather than measured human behaviour (Debug2Fix).

### Cited Findings
- **TraceML (Yan, Sun, Li, Li, Yang; arXiv 2608.26086, v4 Nov 2026) [FULL].** 4,465 human Kaggle trajectories (from public saved notebook versions, Meta Kaggle / Meta Kaggle Code) over 134 competitions; paired subset of 7 competitions with 430 human vs 207 agent trajectories (18 Codex runs, 189 MLEvolve branches; 12-hour budget). Humans pivot on 25% of transitions vs Codex 9% and MLEvolve 58%; top humans return to an earlier approach on 9.1% of eligible versions vs Codex once in 658 and MLEvolve never in 344; Codex recovers 89.0% of setbacks vs top humans 79.4% and MLEvolve 41.1%; Codex spends 19.7% of mass on ensembling (≈78% of that re-weighting) vs humans 6.3%; debugging-intent share is 11.4 points (vs Codex) and 9.6 points (vs MLEvolve) higher for humans. A human-practice planning prompt improved 5/7 competitions and moved ensembling/early-edit behaviour toward humans but not pivoting or revisiting. Authors caution humans are "a reference distribution and not … a control" (not budget-matched). Labeller reliability: intent κ = 0.611 cross-model; human audit 81% agreement (κ = 0.68). — [TraceML](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.26086)
- **Ceka et al., "Understanding Automated Program Repair Agents through the Lens of Traceability" (PACMSE / ISSTA; DOI 10.1145/3832285; arXiv 2506.08311) [FULL, first ~100k of ~114k chars].** SWE-bench Verified, 5 agents (Agentless, AutoCodeRover, SWE-Agent, OpenHands, MASAI) on Claude-3.5 and GPT-4o. Human side = developer gold patches only. Mean diff lines: gold 38.7 / 38.0 vs Agentless 22.0 / 22.6, SWE-Agent 101.5 / 245.7, OpenHands 474.2 / 331.2 (Claude / GPT-4o); files edited gold ~1.25 vs OpenHands-Claude 5.18. Non-clones (no Type-1–3 clone relation to the gold patch) make up 43–68% of *successful* patches, and similarity falls with difficulty. Developers add no reproducer files to patches; agents do. The paper explicitly does not analyse developer workflows. — [arXiv 2506.08311](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.08311)
- **DePro (Parvez, Pallab, Imran, Zaman; FSE '26 Companion; arXiv 2603.19399) [FULL].** 10 Codeforces problems × 10 faulty user submissions; "ChatGPT-5's reasoning model" asked to repair users' code. Humans averaged 3.68 attempts vs 1.8 for the LLM; two competitive programmers rated human–LLM approach similarity 1–5 (range 2.7–5 per problem). Qualitatively, the LLM converged on a single standard template and made localized edits around failing tests, sometimes repeating ineffective fixes or reintroducing errors; humans tested boundary cases, reasoned backward or restructured. Small, non-repository, and the paper has internal numeric inconsistencies (64% "up to" vs average). — [arXiv 2603.19399](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.19399)
- **Debug2Fix (Garg & Huang, Microsoft; arXiv 2602.18571) [FULL via text extraction].** Claims popular agents rely on static reading or test-fix cycles "akin to trial and error debugging" and print-debugging, whereas "expert human developers have long relied on debuggers" — an asserted contrast, with no human data collected. When debug tools were exposed directly to the main agent, it called one in only 17 (9%) of instances (resolving 14 of those vs 11 in baseline); debugging subsequences were ≤4 tool calls. Default Debug-Subagent call rates were 60–70% in Java but diverged by model in Python (the authors hypothesise models are confident fixing Python statically/via print-debugging). — [arXiv 2602.18571](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.18571v1)
  - Cross-check: Alaboudi & LaToza [FULL] measured that professional developers' state inspection was 70% logging and 30% debugger, so "humans use debuggers, agents print-debug" overstates the human side. — [Alaboudi & LaToza](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/2105.02162)
- **"Do AI Coding Agents Log Like Humans?" (Ouatiti, Sayagh, Li, Hassan; arXiv 2604.09409) [ABS].** 4,550 agentic PRs in 81 repos vs human baselines: agents changed logging less often than humans in 58.4% of repos but with higher log density when they did; agents ignored constructive logging requests 67% of the time; humans made 72.5% of post-generation log repairs. Artifact-level, not trajectory-level. — [arXiv 2604.09409](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.09409)
- **SWE-chat (Baumann et al., COLM 2026; arXiv 2604.20779) [ABS].** ~18,000 real coding-agent sessions, >229k prompts, 2M tool calls; authorship bimodal (41% of sessions agents write virtually all committed code; 25% humans write all); only 59% of agent code survives into commits; users corrected/interrupted agents in 50% of turns. Human–agent *collaboration* data, not a side-by-side process comparison. — [arXiv 2604.20779](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.20779)
- **Search-snippet-only leads (not verified):** debug-gym (arXiv 2503.21557) states agents do not yet use debugging tools meaningfully and that human debugging-trace data is scarce and costly; "Comparing Human and LLM Generated Code: The Jury is Still Out!" (arXiv 2501.16857) compares code artifacts, not process; "Dissecting the SWE-Bench Leaderboards" (arXiv 2506.17208) reportedly finds agent patches small and localized, diverging from developers on complex multi-location issues; a CogSci paper comparing human think-aloud with GPT-4o CoT on Zendo tasks reportedly found humans had broader state diversity and more cross-state transitions while GPT-4o showed more repeated local loops ([eScholarship](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/66q0057x)) — all [ABS / snippet only].

### Inferences
- Recurring direction across independent studies (TraceML, DePro, the Zendo CogSci paper, Ceka's patch scope): agents show *narrower* search over approaches (fewer pivots or template convergence, local loops, no return to abandoned approaches) than humans, while being comparable or better at local recovery (Codex setback recovery; fewer attempts in DePro). Patch scope deviates in both directions by scaffold: workflow agents under-scope, open-process agents over-scope relative to developers.
- There is no published human-trajectory comparator on SWE-bench-style issues; openbench would be first to put agent navigation next to human navigation on the *same* issues, but would need to collect or adopt human data (section 4).

### Gaps
- Found no study comparing human and agent fault-localization *navigation* (file-visit sequences) on the same repository issues.
- Found no study comparing when humans vs agents give up / stop, on any software task (TraceML pivots are the closest).
- Did not read 2506.17208, debug-gym, or the Zendo paper in full.

## 4. Datasets of human developer traces on real bug-fix tasks

### Takeaway
Usable human-process comparators exist but none is on SWE-bench issues: the Alaboudi & LaToza coded live-stream dataset (public videos + codes, no stated dataset licence), DBGBench (27 real C bugs with practitioner diagnoses/patches), TraceML (human Kaggle version histories, released with a converter for CLI agent runs), and SWE-chat (real human–agent sessions). Issue-to-fix human time on SWE-bench exists only as annotator estimates or METR-style baselines, not as traces.

### Cited Findings
- **Alaboudi & LaToza debugging-episode dataset**: 15 sessions / 11 pros / ~30 h, coded activities; replication package https://figshare.com/s/6c4026a941db49ea3de3 and dataset link https://bit.ly/3kkbL2W; "The entire dataset, including the videos and codes, is publicly available"; no explicit licence. [FULL] — [arXiv 2105.02162](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/2105.02162)
- **DBGBench**: fault locations, diagnoses and patches for 27 real errors in open-source C projects, consolidated from practitioners' debugging sessions; intended for user studies of debugging aids. Licence not checked. [ABS] — [CISPA record](https://publications.cispa.saarland/1468)
- **TraceML**: 4,465 human trajectories / 134 Kaggle competitions; corpus, schema, labelling models and a toolkit converting "a run from any command-line agent" into TraceML format released (licence not checked). ML-competition work, not bug fixing. [FULL] — [arXiv 2608.26086](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.26086)
- **SWE-chat**: ~18k real sessions with tool calls and diffs attributed to human vs agent; "living dataset"; paper CC BY 4.0, dataset access terms not stated on the abstract page. [ABS] — [arXiv 2604.20779](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.20779)
- **Ko 2006 / Lawrance 2013 transcripts**: not stated as released in what I read. [FULL] — [Ko](https://faculty.washington.edu/ajko/papers/Ko2006SeekRelateCollect.pdf), [Lawrance](https://web.engr.oregonstate.edu/~burnett/Reprints/TSE-IFT-2013-asprinted.pdf)
- **Human time baselines**: METR's time-horizon method uses human baseliner completion times per task; critics note baseliners are unfamiliar with the codebase and familiarisation time handling matters for short tasks. A secondary claim of "over 800 human baseline runs, 2,529 hours" is unverified. [ABS] — [METR blog](https://metr.org/blog/2025-03-19-measuring-ai-ability-to-complete-long-tasks/)
- **SWE-bench Verified human annotation** (OpenAI, 2024: annotators rated issue clarity, test validity and estimated fix time in buckets): the OpenAI page returned HTTP 403, so I could not verify annotator counts, buckets or release location. — [OpenAI page (not retrieved)](https://openai.com/index/introducing-swe-bench-verified/)

### Inferences
- The Alaboudi & LaToza codes (browse / edit / test / inspect-state / consult / other) map almost one-to-one onto agent command classes and are the cheapest human comparator to adopt as a shared coding scheme.
- TraceML's converter is the only released tool built specifically to put CLI-agent runs and human trajectories in one schema; its version-level (not command-level) granularity would need adapting to bug fixing.
- A SWE-bench-specific human comparator would have to be collected (e.g. screen-recorded developers on a subset of openbench tasks) or approximated by mining upstream fix PR histories (commit sequences, review rounds) — not found as an existing corpus.

- Not searched: KaVE/Enriched Event Streams (MSR 2018 IDE interaction dataset), Mylyn interaction histories, Blaze/FeedBaG — known in the field but unverified here.

## 5. Pitfalls in mapping human cognitive constructs onto agent traces

### Takeaway
The human constructs were defined from think-aloud plus time-coded behaviour of people with working memory, fatigue and IDE costs; agent traces have none of these, are step-counted, and their reasoning text is not a concurrent verbal report. The safest transfers are action-defined constructs (navigation, revisits, failed searches, reproduction, test cadence); text-defined constructs (hypotheses, scent talk) should be presence-only and compared as structure, not wording.

### Cited Findings
- Even in humans, verbalizations were a *worse* predictor of navigation than the bug report text (Lawrance), and IFT was proposed precisely to avoid relying on unobservable mental states like hypotheses. — [Lawrance et al. 2013](https://web.engr.oregonstate.edu/~burnett/Reprints/TSE-IFT-2013-asprinted.pdf)
- A large share of human navigation cost in Ko 2006 is IDE mechanics (tab loss, scroll search, abbreviated filenames, "wrap search" unchecked); these costs have no agent analogue, so the 35% navigation figure is partly an interface artefact. — [Ko et al. 2006](https://faculty.washington.edu/ajko/papers/Ko2006SeekRelateCollect.pdf)
- Human-vs-agent process comparisons are not budget-matched; TraceML explicitly treats humans as a reference distribution rather than a control, and its human traces see only publicly saved versions (off-platform work invisible). — [TraceML](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.26086)
- Claims about human practice in agent papers can be asserted rather than measured: Debug2Fix's "experts use debuggers" contrast vs measured 70% logging / 30% debugger among professionals (Alaboudi & LaToza). — [Debug2Fix](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.18571v1); [Alaboudi & LaToza](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/2105.02162)
- Human gold patches are not a process trace: Ceka et al. state that their human comparison is limited to patches and benchmark test labels. — [Ceka et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.08311)
- Think-aloud vs CoT: a CogSci study comparing human think-aloud with GPT-4o CoT on Zendo found different transition structure (humans broader, GPT-4o more local loops) [ABS]; a 2025 preprint (arXiv 2511.16660, "Cognitive Foundations" per the openbench map) annotated LLM traces alongside 54 human think-aloud traces [ABS]. — [eScholarship](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/66q0057x); [arXiv 2511.16660](https://api.emergentmind.com/papers/2511.16660)

### Inferences
- Think-aloud is a concurrent report elicited under prompting ("please keep talking"), with silences; model reasoning text is generated output that is trained, not elicited, and (per the openbench map §5b) has low and variable faithfulness. Word-level matches ("let me check", "I suspect") should not be treated as evidence of the same construct.
- Human studies measure minutes; agents spend tokens and steps, with near-zero navigation cost per file. Ratios like "35% navigating" should be compared as rank-orders or as conditional structure (e.g. revisits after an edit), not as matched percentages.
- Human samples are small (5–12 developers in the key lab studies), Java/Eclipse-era, and on 1-hour unfamiliar-code tasks; treat them as construct definitions and effect directions, not as population norms.
- Expertise is a confound for any "human-like" label: the expert/novice literature (Gugerty & Olson) and the systematic/as-needed literature (Littman) define *which* humans an agent resembles; the comparator should name the human reference group.

### Gaps
- No source found that specifically analyses the validity of applying Ericsson & Simon-style protocol-analysis assumptions to LLM reasoning text in agentic coding; the cautions above beyond the cited findings are my inference.
